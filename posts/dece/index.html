DeCE: a review

September 7, 2026 · 5 min read

1. The paper#

DeCE is an evaluation framework for an LLM judge. Instead of one pointwise score per answer, the judge reports precision and recall, each computed from criteria extracted from an expert's gold standard answer. Yu, Seedat, Herrmannova, Schilder and Schwarz at Thomson Reuters, EMNLP 2025 Industry Track.

The setting is legal QA: long-form answers that have to cite the statutes, regulations and cases that apply. DeCE generates the checklist for each question from the gold standard answer.

2. The method#

Each task is a question, a model answer, and a gold standard answer written by an expert in two sections: Required Information, the content that governs the question, and Helpful Information, supporting authorities.

Recall is computed from the Required Information only. The judge turns it into a checklist of criteria, marks each 1 or 0 against the model answer, and recall is the fraction satisfied. Coverage of what must be there.

Precision is computed against the whole gold standard answer. The judge breaks the model answer into claims, marks each 1 or 0 on whether the gold standard answer supports it, and precision is the fraction supported. How much of what the model said the expert would stand behind.

Criteria are generated per question, not from a fixed rubric. Claude 3.5 Sonnet does every step. The headline metric is F2, recall weighted twice as much as precision.

3. Results#

They test DeCE on 224 legal questions, each with an expert's gold standard answer. Five models answer every question: GPT-4o, Gemini 2.5 Pro, DeepSeek-R1, Llama 3.1 405B, and a legal fine-tune of Llama 3.1 70B that Thomson Reuters trained in-house.

To check whether DeCE agrees with humans, four legal experts scored model answers themselves, one expert per answer. They did this in two ways. For 100 answers (20 questions, all five models) they scored precision and recall the way DeCE does. For all 224 GPT-4o answers they gave a single overall score out of 4, the way a pointwise judge does (the paper's Table 3 lists that count as 244). Then each automatic metric is correlated with the matching human score. The comparison metrics are a pointwise LLM judge, two multidimensional judges (GPTScore and G-Eval) and RAGChecker, a claim-checking method.

The headline is the F2 column: DeCE correlates with the experts at 0.78, against 0.35 to 0.48 for everything else. That is a big gap and it is real.

The next two columns say where it comes from. On recall, DeCE gets 0.80 and the others get 0.39 to 0.56, so the criteria extracted from the Required Information track what experts count as complete far better than generic criteria do. On precision, DeCE gets 0.69 and GPTScore also gets 0.69, so the claim-checking half of DeCE is no better than an existing judge. All of the gain is recall.

The last column is the one to hold in mind. When experts give one overall score instead of scoring in DeCE's terms, the plain pointwise judge agrees with them better than DeCE does, 0.59 to 0.46. DeCE matches experts best when the experts are asked to think like DeCE.

Two more results. The criteria the judge writes are good: experts reviewed all 979 of them and changed 11.95%, dropped 0.7% and added 2.0%. And the models trade off: the two reasoning models, Gemini 2.5 Pro and DeepSeek-R1, cover nearly everything required (recall near 1) but only about 42% of what they say is supported by the gold standard answer, while the legal fine-tune is the most precise and covers the least.

(One footnote: the paper gives G-Eval F2 as 0.42 in its Table 2 and 0.48 in its Table 3; the table above uses Table 3.)

4. My thoughts#

The Required and Helpful split is a great contribution to rubric design. It encodes an inductive bias: that in this domain some content governs the answer and some only supports it. The expert supplies that bias just by writing the gold standard answer in two sections. In the right setting, a domain where that distinction is real, it improves grader correctness, and the results show it: recall against the Required criteria tracks experts at 0.80 where the generic frameworks get 0.53 to 0.56. In a setting where the distinction is not real, the same bias is a distortion.

The two metrics have synergy. Precision rewards saying less, recall rewards saying more, and each caps the other's exploit.

However the gold standard answer cannot be exhausted. "Supported" means the expert wrote it down, not that it is true, so a valid authority the expert did not list scores 0. The paper proposes two fixes and I am not convinced by either: flexible authority matching asks the verifier to make the legal judgement the reference was there to avoid, and human-in-the-loop promotion, even with an expert signing off each addition, lets recurring model claims shape the Helpful pool that later models are scored against.

On the correlation, the 0.78 is DeCE agreeing with experts asked to think in DeCE's terms, and on the unframed score the pointwise judge is ahead. F2 was chosen with the numbers in hand, length is never reported, and a correlation controlling for answer length is the one analysis I would want.

So this is a great framework where the rubric design fits: expert domains with required content and a hierarchy of supporting material, law, medicine, compliance. There it encodes the right inductive bias and it correlates better with experts.

Worth reading if you build graders for expert work.
