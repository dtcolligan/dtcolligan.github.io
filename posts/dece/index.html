DeCE: a review

September 7, 2026 · 4 min read

1. The paper#

DeCE is an evaluation framework for an LLM judge. Instead of one pointwise score per answer, the judge reports precision and recall, each computed from criteria extracted from an expert's gold standard answer. Yu, Seedat, Herrmannova, Schilder and Schwarz at Thomson Reuters, EMNLP 2025 Industry Track.

The setting is legal QA: long-form answers that have to cite the statutes, regulations and cases that apply. DeCE generates the checklist for each question from the gold standard answer.

2. The method#

Each task is a question, a model answer, and a gold standard answer written by an expert in two sections: Required Information, the content that governs the question, and Helpful Information, supporting authorities.

Recall is computed from the Required Information only. The judge turns it into a checklist of criteria, marks each 1 or 0 against the model answer, and recall is the fraction satisfied. Coverage of what must be there.

Precision is computed against the whole gold standard answer. The judge breaks the model answer into claims, marks each 1 or 0 on whether the gold standard answer supports it, and precision is the fraction supported. How much of what the model said the expert would stand behind.

Criteria are generated per question, not from a fixed rubric. Claude 3.5 Sonnet does every step. The headline metric is F2, recall weighted twice as much as precision.

3. Results#

224 US legal QA pairs, five models (GPT-4o, Gemini 2.5 Pro, DeepSeek-R1, Llama 3.1 405B, an in-house legal fine-tune of Llama 3.1 70B). Four legal experts, one annotator per item. Two human targets: 100 answers scored as precision and recall, and all 224 GPT-4o answers given one overall score out of 4 (their Table 3 lists that count as 244; the text says 224).

Three things to read off it. (One footnote: the paper gives G-Eval F2 as 0.42 in its Table 2 and 0.48 in its Table 3; the table above uses Table 3.) DeCE's F2 correlates at 0.78 with experts scoring in DeCE's own terms, the best in the table by a margin. The gain is entirely recall, 0.80 against 0.53 to 0.56; on precision it ties GPTScore. Against the experts' overall score, the plain pointwise judge wins, 0.59 to 0.46.

The criteria hold up: of 979 generated, 11.95% were modified by experts, 0.7% discarded, 2.0% added. And the models trade off: the two reasoning models, Gemini 2.5 Pro and DeepSeek-R1, sit near recall 1 with precision around 0.42, while the legal fine-tune has the highest precision at the cost of recall.

4. My thoughts#

The Required and Helpful split is a great contribution to rubric design. The expert encodes an inductive bias about the domain, what governs and what supports, just by writing the gold standard answer in two sections, and it improves grader correctness: recall against the Required criteria tracks experts at 0.80 where the generic frameworks get 0.53 to 0.56.

The two metrics have synergy. Precision rewards saying less, recall rewards saying more, and each caps the other's exploit.

However the gold standard answer cannot be exhausted. "Supported" means the expert wrote it down, not that it is true, so a valid authority the expert did not list scores 0. The paper proposes two fixes and I am not convinced by either: flexible authority matching asks the verifier to make the legal judgement the reference was there to avoid, and human-in-the-loop promotion, even with an expert signing off each addition, lets recurring model claims shape the Helpful pool that later models are scored against.

On the correlation, the 0.78 is DeCE agreeing with experts asked to think in DeCE's terms, and on the unframed score the pointwise judge is ahead. F2 was chosen with the numbers in hand, length is never reported, and a correlation controlling for answer length is the one analysis I would want.

So this is a great framework where the rubric design fits: expert domains with required content and a hierarchy of supporting material, law, medicine, compliance. There it encodes the right inductive bias and it correlates better with experts. Outside that shape the split has nothing to bite on.

Worth reading if you build graders for expert work.
